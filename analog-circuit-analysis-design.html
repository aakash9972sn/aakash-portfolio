01 / INSTITUTIONAL CONTEXT

Bridging Academic Theory with
Industry-Standard Practice.

Organized by the Department of Electronics and Communication Engineering at Delhi Technological University.

Delhi Technological University (formerly Delhi College of Engineering), situated in Shahbad Daulatpur, Bawana Road, Delhi, stands as one of India's premier technological institutions. Established in 1941, DTU plays a pivotal role in the national and global innovation network, cultivating methodical engineering research and rigorous technical excellence.

The Department of Electronics and Communication Engineering (ECE), established in 1976, has spent five decades nurturing innovations in semiconductor devices, analog/digital circuit design, signal processing, and communication systems. The department's mission emphasizes creating a continuously evolving educational environment that tackles tomorrow's hardware challenges.

This two-day workshop was specifically conceived to bridge the persistent divide between classroom circuit analysis and real-world hardware synthesis. Rather than approaching analog electronics as a tedious collection of Kirchhoff's laws and giant determinants, the curriculum introduced students to Design-Oriented Analysis (DOA)—equipping future circuit designers with the intuition required for advanced laboratory courses, core-industry placements, and hardware research.

03 / CORE METHODOLOGY

The Philosophy of
Design-Oriented Analysis (DOA).

Why traditional textbook analysis fails when engineering real systems, and how low-entropy thinking unlocks hardware design.

Traditional Circuit Analysis

Given Circuit Topology → Find Outputs

In conventional classroom analysis, the circuit diagram, all component values ($R_1, R_2, C, \beta$), and source characteristics are completely known beforehand.

  • • Determined System: $N_{\text{equations}} = N_{\text{variables}}$. Solvable by mechanical application of KCL/KVL or Cramer's rule.
  • • Brute-Force Algebra: Produces monstrous polynomials where individual component values are entangled and obscured.
  • • Zero Design Insight: If the gain is too low or bandwidth too narrow, the equations give no clue which resistor or capacitor to adjust.
Engineering Circuit Design (DOA)

Given Specifications → Synthesize Circuit

In engineering design, you begin with a blank sheet and target performance specifications: gain $A_v$, bandwidth $f_{\text{cutoff}}$, input impedance $R_{\text{in}}$, and dynamic range.

  • • Under-Constrained System: $N_{\text{variables}} > N_{\text{equations}}$. There are infinite possible component combinations.
  • • Engineering Choices: The designer must make informed choices using rules of thumb, trade-offs, standard E24 series values, and thermal margins.
  • • Low-Entropy Form: Expressions must be written so that every parameter corresponds directly to a physical circuit element or asymptotic corner.

"Do Not Cross-Multiply Stuff!" — Middlebrook's Rule of Low-Entropy

Lecture Note Sheet 2
High-Entropy Expression (Avoid)
H(s) = \frac{a_0 + a_1 s + a_2 s^2}{b_0 + b_1 s + b_2 s^2 + b_3 s^3}

A high-entropy polynomial has all coefficients expanded out. It hides where the poles and zeros are located, making it impossible to see which capacitor limits high-frequency response or how to adjust feedback.

Low-Entropy Expression (Design-Oriented)
H(s) = A_0 \cdot \frac{1 + \frac{s}{\omega_z}}{\left(1 + \frac{s}{\omega_{p1}}\right)\left(1 + \frac{s}{\omega_{p2}}\right)}

A low-entropy expression expresses the transfer function in terms of midband reference gain $A_0$ and salient corner frequencies ($\omega_z, \omega_p$). Any shift in a component immediately shows its exact effect on the Bode plot!

✦ Terminal Resistance & Dominance Principles (Sheets 3 & 4)

A key skill in DOA is finding the equivalent resistance seen by any capacitor or node without redrawing large small-signal networks:

  • • Parallel Dominance: When two or more resistive paths exist in parallel ($R_1 \parallel R_2$), the smallest resistance strictly dominates the equivalent impedance ($R_{\text{eq}} \approx R_{\text{small}}$).
  • • Series Dominance: When impedances are in series ($R_1 + R_2$), the largest resistance dominates ($R_{\text{eq}} \approx R_{\text{large}}$).
  • • Source Loading Effect: An ideal voltage source has zero output impedance ($R_s = 0$). Real-world signal generators and microphones have finite $R_s$, attenuating the signal if the amplifier's input impedance $R_{\text{in}}$ is not engineered significantly higher than $R_s$.
04 / BIPOLAR JUNCTION TRANSISTORS

Thermal Sensitivity & Negative Feedback:
The Emitter Degeneration ($R_E$) Solution.

Analyzing why fixed base-biasing fails catastrophically, and how the 10% $V_{CC}$ rule establishes rock-solid operating points.

The Thermal Instability Trap

The Exponential $I_C - V_{BE}$ Relation

In a bipolar junction transistor, collector current follows the Shockley diode equation:

I_C = I_S \exp\left(\frac{V_{BE}}{V_T}\right) \iff V_{BE} = V_T \ln\left(\frac{I_C}{I_S}\right)

Where $V_T = \frac{kT}{q} \approx 26\text{ mV}$ at room temperature. Because of this exponential relationship:

  • • $V_{BE}$ Temperature Drift: $V_{BE}$ drops by approximately $-2\text{ mV}/^\circ\text{C}$ as the transistor heats up.
  • • The 300% Surge Catastrophe: A mere $10\%$ decrease in $V_{BE}$ (or slight temperature rise) causes an explosive $+300\%$ surge in collector current $I_C$!
  • • Thermal Runaway: Without stabilization, rising $I_C$ generates internal $I^2 R$ heat, which increases temperature, further increasing $I_C$ until the transistor melts or saturates.
The Negative Feedback Cure

Emitter Degeneration ($R_E$) Feedback

Inserting an emitter resistor $R_E$ introduces series-series negative feedback that self-regulates the collector current:

I_C \uparrow \implies V_E = I_E R_E \uparrow \implies V_{BE} = (V_B - V_E) \downarrow \implies I_C \downarrow

The loop gain automatically stabilizes the operating point:

  • • $\beta$-Insensitive Bias: Collector current becomes fixed primarily by passive resistors: $I_C \approx \frac{V_B - 0.7\text{V}}{R_E}$.
  • • The Industry 10% Rule of Thumb: Allocate $10\%$ of rail supply across $R_E$ ($V_{RE} \approx 0.1 V_{CC}$). For $V_{CC}=20\text{V}$, $V_{RE}=2\text{V}$; for $I_C=10\text{mA}$, $R_E=200\ \Omega$.
  • • Base Divider Stiffening: Design divider current $I_{\text{divider}} \ge 10 I_B$ so base voltage $V_B$ is immune to loading, then match to standard E24 laboratory resistors (e.g. 47k and 8.2k).

05 / CIRCUIT SYNTHESIS

Small-Signal Audio Amplifier:
Inspection-Based Gain & Capacitor Sizing.

Deriving midband voltage gain, input impedance, and coupling capacitor boundaries without solving matrix determinants.

Small-Signal Parameters

Dynamic Emitter Resistance ($r_e$)

Looking into the emitter terminal of a forward-biased BJT reveals an intrinsic AC dynamic resistance governed strictly by thermal voltage and DC bias current:

r_e = \frac{V_T}{I_C} \approx \frac{26\text{ mV}}{10\text{ mA}} = 2.6\ \Omega

Because $r_e$ is remarkably small ($2.6\ \Omega$), the intrinsic transconductance $g_m = 1/r_e \approx 385\text{ mS}$ provides tremendous voltage gain capability when un-degenerated.

Gain by Inspection

The Complete Midband Transfer Function

Using Design-Oriented Analysis, the voltage gain from source $v_s$ to output $v_o$ is written down in a single glance:

A_v = \frac{v_o}{v_s} = -\left(\frac{R_B}{R_s + R_B}\right) \cdot \frac{\alpha R_C}{r_e + R_E + \frac{R_s \parallel R_B}{1+\beta}}

Where $R_B = R_1 \parallel R_2$ and $\alpha = \frac{\beta}{1+\beta} \approx 0.99$. The leading factor accounts for input source attenuation, while the denominator transparently sums all resistances in the emitter loop!

✦ Input Coupling Capacitor ($C_1$) Sizing & The $f_1 / 10$ Safety Rule (Sheets 12 & 13)

The input coupling capacitor $C_1$ isolates the sensitive DC base bias from external audio sources while passing AC audio signals. Sizing $C_1$ requires calculating the total resistance in its loop:

R_{\text{in}} = R_B \parallel \left[(1+\beta)(r_e + R_E)\right] \implies f_{p1} = \frac{1}{2\pi C_1 (R_s + R_{\text{in}})}

The 10x Safety Rule: If the lowest audio frequency of interest is $f_1 = 100\text{ Hz}$ (or $20\text{ Hz}$ for high-fidelity bass), never set the $-3\text{ dB}$ corner right at $f_1$. At the corner frequency, the signal is attenuated by $3\text{ dB}$ ($30\%$) and suffers a massive $45^\circ$ phase distortion. Instead, the workshop taught to design $f_{corner} = f_1 / 10 = 10\text{ Hz}$, ensuring $< 0.04\text{ dB}$ attenuation and zero perceptible phase shift in the audio band!

06 / ADVANCED CIRCUIT THEOREMS

Middlebrook's Extra Element Theorem (EET):
Adding Components Without Re-Solving.

One of the most powerful theorems in analog design: modify any circuit with an extra impedance $Z$ using driving-point and null double-injection impedances.

⚡ The Master EET Formulation

If a circuit transfer function $A\big|_{Z\to\infty}$ is known when element $Z$ is absent (open circuit), the exact transfer function with $Z$ connected is given by:

A(s) = A\Big|_{Z\to\infty} \cdot \frac{1 + \frac{Z_n}{Z}}{1 + \frac{Z_d}{Z}}

Where $Z_d$ is the driving-point impedance seen by $Z$ when input $v_s = 0$, and $Z_n$ is the null double-injection impedance seen by $Z$ when output $v_o$ is held at null ($v_o \equiv 0$).

🔍 Calculating $Z_d$ and $Z_n$ by Inspection

Applying EET to the Emitter Bypass Capacitor ($C_2$) across $R_E$:

  • Driving Point Impedance ($Z_d$): With input shorted ($v_s=0$), looking into $R_E$ terminals reveals $Z_d = R_E \parallel r_e'$.
  • Null Injection Impedance ($Z_n$): Under the condition $v_o = 0$, collector current $i_c = 0 \implies i_e = 0$. No current enters the emitter; hence $Z$ sees only $Z_n = R_E$!
\omega_z = \frac{1}{C_2 R_E}, \quad \omega_p = \frac{1}{C_2 (R_E \parallel r_e')}

Interactive Bode Magnitude Response & Pole-Zero Shelf

Observe how the Extra Element Theorem creates a low-frequency shelf (governed by $R_E$) and transitions to high-gain midband (governed by $r_e$).

Zero Frequency ($f_z = \omega_z/2\pi$): 17 Hz Pole Frequency ($f_p = \omega_p/2\pi$): 1.3 kHz Midband AC Gain: ~51.7 dB

✦ Dual Extra Element Theorem (DEET) for Series Elements (Sheet 17)

When adding a series element (such as an input coupling capacitor $C_1$), the natural reference condition is a short circuit ($Z \to 0$) rather than an open circuit. Middlebrook formulated the Dual EET:

A(s) = A\Big|_{Z\to 0} \cdot \frac{1 + \frac{Z}{Z_n^*}}{1 + \frac{Z}{Z_d^*}}

Where $Z_d^*$ and $Z_n^*$ represent the driving-point and null short-circuit impedances across the cut port, providing seamless duality for series vs parallel circuit modifications.

08 / ENGINEERING REFLECTIONS

Four Foundational Insights
from the Workshop.

Personal takeaways from experiencing Design-Oriented Analysis as an undergraduate engineering student at DTU.

TAKEAWAY 01

Design is an Under-Constrained Craft

Classroom problem-solving often tricks students into believing circuits have a single predetermined algebraic answer. In reality, design is inherently under-constrained ($N_{\text{variables}} > N_{\text{equations}}$). Mastery lies in making intelligent engineering choices—sizing bias networks with the 10% rule, preventing source loading, and selecting available standard E24 components.

TAKEAWAY 02

Low-Entropy Expressions Save Sanity

Blindly multiplying out polynomials produces algebraic clutter that buries circuit insight. Keeping transfer functions in low-entropy factored pole-zero format means that every single term in the equation corresponds directly to a physical component or frequency shelf. If an amplifier fails a spec, you immediately know which resistor or capacitor to adjust.

TAKEAWAY 03

Negative Feedback is Nature's Autopilot

Understanding the exponential $I_C = I_S e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ sensitivity demonstrated why simple base resistors lead to thermal runaway. Adding emitter degeneration ($R_E$) turns the circuit into a self-regulating feedback system: as current tries to surge, emitter voltage rises, choking $V_{BE}$ and pinning the operating point with unwavering stability.

TAKEAWAY 04

Middlebrook's EET as an Analytical Superpower

Instead of resolving entire KCL matrices when a bypass capacitor or feedback resistor is introduced, the Extra Element Theorem allows calculating driving-point ($Z_d$) and null double-injection ($Z_n$) impedances by inspection. It turns what would be three pages of painful algebra into a clean, intuitive transfer function.
